\section{Introduction}
Prime editing uses a programmable guide RNA and a reverse-transcriptase-containing editor to install substitutions, insertions and deletions without requiring a donor DNA template or a double-strand break \citep{anzalone2019}. For a specified edit, alternative spacers, primer-binding sites (PBSs), reverse-transcription templates (RTTs) and guide constructs can produce different editing efficiencies. Predictive models therefore have two related but distinct uses: estimating the outcome of an individual design, and selecting a design from a feasible candidate set.

Existing approaches address important parts of this problem. PRIDICT predicts editing efficiency and product purity; DeepPrime models diverse editing systems and cellular settings; PRIDICT2.0 and ePRIDICT extend prediction across chromatin contexts; and OPED uses transfer learning for guide prediction and design \citep{mathis2023,yu2023,mathis2025,liu2023oped}. OptiPrime connects learned sequence-dependent quantities to a mechanistic model of prime-editing outcomes \citep{hsu2026}. These studies motivate both improved sequence representations and careful evaluation of what a prediction advantage means for a design decision. Our experiments compare against the released OptiPrime system on explicitly defined populations; they do not reevaluate every application or biological claim in the OptiPrime study.

A pooled correlation can improve because a model distinguishes easy from difficult loci, studies or experimental conditions. None of these differences necessarily helps a user who has already fixed the desired allele, cell type and editor. Even a within-locus correlation can mix distinct edits or conditions when the grouping key is too broad. Conversely, an accurate estimate of each candidate's conditional mean outcome is sufficient for optimal top-one selection under an expected-efficiency objective: a separate ranking branch or candidate-set architecture is not mathematically required. The empirical question is whether a training procedure learns the relevant ordering under the distribution encountered at deployment.

We developed \model{} as a comparatively direct sequence-to-outcome model and progressively evaluated it beyond pooled prediction. A paired edit representation makes nucleotide changes explicit, while a separate segment-aware pegRNA stream and cross-attention expose relationships between the guide and its target. We compared self-attention with diagonal state-space mixing and scalar, proportion and cumulative-threshold heads. This development produced a strong source-benchmark ensemble, but a subsequent external-library analysis revealed a reversal in top-choice utility despite a retained correlation advantage.

We then investigated candidate grouping, optimization, target supervision and retention of source-domain decisions. The study is entirely computational: all editing outcomes were previously measured in the source publications or supplied corpus. Its central contribution is an integrated account of when prediction improvements translate into useful choices, which proposed repairs survive matched controls, and what source-preserving adaptation costs on the target task. We report successful, negative and corrected analyses together. Because the research proceeded adaptively, we distinguish the initially reserved external comparison from subsequent development on that same library, and we do not describe the resulting low-label experiments as independent confirmation.
